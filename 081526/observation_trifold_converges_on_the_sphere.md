# The tri-fold tent converges on the sphere — in volume, not in surface

**Date:** 2026-08-15
**Tool:** `fd-lattice/oct-sphere.html` (localhost:8090/oct-sphere.html), scrubbable
0–10, both schemes, live metrics
**Status:** measured. Volume convergence is clean and geometric. The surface
result is a separate, weaker statement and is stated separately on purpose.

---

## The construction

Take a regular octahedron with unit edges, inscribed so all six vertices lie on
the sphere. Circumradius **R = 1/√2 = 0.7071068**, sphere volume **1.4809610**.

One level = tent **every** triangle at once:

1. take the triangle's centroid,
2. push it radially until it meets the sphere,
3. the triangle becomes three, fanned from that apex.

Counts go **8·3ⁿ**: 8, 24, 72, 216, 648, 1944, …

**The first tent height is exact.** The centroid of a face sits at the
octahedron's inradius 1/√6, and the apex lands at R, so

    h₁ = 1/√2 − 1/√6 = (3√2 − √6)/6 = 0.2988584907…

verified numerically to 15 digits. For the first tent, and only the first, the
radial push is also the perpendicular height above the face — the face is
symmetric about its own normal. Deeper tents are pitched on tilted slopes, and
there the two measures separate: by level 10 the true perpendicular height is
**29.6%** of the radial push, with the slope tilted ~28.5°.

## It converges. Here is the volume.

Computed exactly — signed tetrahedron sum `(1/6)|Σ v₀·(v₁×v₂)|` over the closed
surface, no sampling:

| level | triangles | volume | % of sphere | deficit | deficit ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 8 | 0.47140452 | 31.831% | 1.00955646 | — |
| 1 | 24 | 0.81649658 | 55.133% | 0.66446440 | 0.658 |
| 2 | 72 | 1.06304796 | 71.781% | 0.41791302 | 0.629 |
| 3 | 216 | 1.22566118 | 82.761% | 0.25529980 | 0.611 |
| 4 | 648 | 1.32826489 | 89.689% | 0.15269609 | 0.598 |
| 5 | 1944 | 1.39108572 | 93.931% | 0.08987526 | 0.589 |
| 6 | 5832 | 1.42871236 | 96.472% | 0.05224862 | 0.581 |
| 7 | 17496 | 1.45087836 | 97.969% | 0.03008262 | 0.576 |
| 8 | 52488 | 1.46377141 | 98.839% | 0.01718957 | 0.571 |
| 9 | 157464 | 1.47119713 | 99.341% | 0.00976385 | 0.568 |
| 10 | 472392 | 1.47544110 | 99.627% | 0.00551988 | 0.565 |

**The deficit falls by a settling factor of ~0.56 every level.** The sequence is
monotone increasing (every tent adds volume and removes none) and bounded above
by the ball (every vertex is on the sphere, so the polyhedron is inscribed), so
it converges; and the deficit is measurably going to zero geometrically rather
than to a positive limit. At level 10 the tri-fold body already encloses
**99.627%** of the sphere.

That is the claim: **the tri-fold method captures the sphere's volume.**

## What does *not* converge, and why that is compatible

The surface does not converge uniformly. Measured at every level:

    longest edge   1.000000     unchanged from level 0
    gap to sphere  0.207107     locked from level 1 onward

Every child triangle is (vertex, vertex, apex), so it inherits one **whole**
parent edge. The twelve original octahedron edges are therefore edges of the
surface at every level, forever. Each is a straight chord between two points of
the sphere, and its midpoint sits at radius exactly 0.5 against R = 0.7071 — a
gap of **0.2071** that is identical at level 1 and level ∞.

So the limit is a sphere with twelve straight chords cut through it.

**Both facts are true, and they are not in tension.** The discrepancy lives on
twelve one-dimensional arcs. A curve has no volume. The slivers crowding against
those arcs get thinner without bound, filling the space while never straightening
the chord. Enclosing the same region and being the same surface are different
claims:

* **volume / L¹** — converges. The tri-fold body → the ball.
* **surface / L∞** — does not. sup-distance stays 0.2071.

Stating the second as though it were the first is the error that made this take
three rounds to settle.

---

## Why tri-fold is the octahedrally native one

The four-way scheme (split each edge at its midpoint, project, four children)
converges faster — 99.991% by level 8, gap down to 3.2e-5 — but it does so by
**inventing a new vertex set**. Edge midpoints are not generated by the
octahedron; they are a new lattice laid over it, and after one level the original
geometry is gone.

Tri-fold only ever adds the point the existing triangle already defines: its own
centroid, pushed along its own radius. Every vertex it creates is generated by
vertices already present, through an operation the solid itself supplies. The
octahedral symmetry is preserved at every level — the 48-element signed
permutation group acts on the level-n surface exactly as it acts on the
octahedron.

The price is exactly the twelve creases. The construction refuses to touch the
original edges, so the original edges survive.

## The shape itself

At level 9 — 157,464 triangles — the surface is strikingly fractal, and the
structure is legible:

* **Six radial starbursts**, at the octahedron's vertices. Every triangle that
  ever touched a vertex still touches it, so an unbounded fan of edges converges
  on each. These are genuine singular points of the limit.
* **Twelve bright seams** along the original edges, where the slivers stack.
* **Self-similar branching** inside each face: each centroid apex is itself a
  small starburst, and its children make smaller ones, recursively. This is what
  reads as fractal — the tent hierarchy is visible all the way down.
* **Quiet lens-shaped regions** between the seams, where the surface is smooth
  and already essentially on the sphere.

The fractal texture is not decoration: it is the record of where the measure went.
The bright structure is where the construction is still working, and it is
concentrated on a set of measure zero.

## Open

* **Close the proof.** The numerics show a settling deficit ratio ~0.56; a clean
  argument that the ratio is bounded below 1 would make the convergence a
  theorem rather than a strong measurement. Monotone-and-bounded already gives
  convergence to *something*; what needs pinning is that the limit is the ball.
* **Surface area.** Volume converges; area may not — slivers can inflate it
  without bound. Unmeasured, and it decides whether this is a rectifiable limit.
* **The tilted-slope variant.** Pitching each sub-tent perpendicular to its own
  slope rather than radially is a different construction whose descendants all
  differ. Not yet built.
* **Does the crease matter for FD?** The twelve arcs are exactly the octahedron's
  own edges — the equator and the pole rods. If the physical lattice inherits
  this construction, those are the places where a continuum limit would fail
  first, which is suggestive given how much of the CA's structure lives on them.
